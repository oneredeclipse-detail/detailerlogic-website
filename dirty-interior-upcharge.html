How to charge for a dirty interior

You quoted $225 for an interior. You open the door and it's crushed fries, a spilled latte and sand in every seam. Now it's a 4-hour job at a 2.5-hour price. Here's how we price condition so the dirty ones pay for themselves, and the client still says yes.

Updated November 11, 2026 · 5-minute read

What a dirty interior really costs you

Same mid-size SUV, $75 an hour. A normal interior takes 2.5 hours. A trashed one takes closer to 4.

Quoted: 2.5 hrs × $75 × 1.2 (mid-size SUV)$225
Real time on the job4 hrs
What that job paid you per hour$56

You lost 1.5 hours of pay, about $135, and that's before the extra product, the extra extractor passes and the drying time. Do one of those a week and it's over $6,000 a year.

Condition changes the hours more than size does. Price the condition.

The 4 condition levels

Give every interior one of four levels. Base them on what you can see, so you can point to it when the client asks why.

LevelWhat you seeUpcharge
StandardNormal use. Light crumbs, dust, a few marks. Cleaned in the last few months.None
HeavyFood and drink spills, ground-in dirt in the carpet, stained seats, cup holders caked, no detail in a year or more.+25%
RestoreHeavy stains on most seats and carpet, smoke or strong odor, trash on the floors, salt crust from a winter.+50%
ExtremeMold, vomit, bodily fluids, heavy smoke residue on the headliner, used as a work truck for years.+100%

Starting points. If your heavy jobs run longer, raise your numbers.

The math on each level

Interior only, mid-size SUV, 2.5 hours at $75 an hour:

Standard: 2.5 × $75 × 1.2$225.00
Heavy: × 1.25$281.25
Restore: × 1.5$337.50
Extreme: × 2$450.00

The trashed SUV from the top took 4 hours. Priced as Restore, it's $337.50: about $84 an hour instead of $56, close to the $90 a clean one pays. That's the point. A dirty car should pay you about the same hourly rate as a clean one.

Only charge it on the inside

A trashed interior doesn't make the paint any dirtier. If you add a heavy-soil charge to the whole job, the exterior gets marked up for no reason, and that's the number that makes a client shop around.

Interior: 2.5 hrs × $75 × 1.2 × 1.25 (heavy)$281.25
Exterior: 2 hrs × $75 × 1.2 (normal)$180.00
Total, priced by part$461.25

Charge heavy on the whole job and it's $506.25. That extra $45 isn't for any extra work, and a client who asks "why?" deserves a straight answer. Price each side by its own condition and you have one.

Pet hair is its own line

Don't fold pet hair into the condition level. A spotless interior can still have a husky's winter coat woven into the carpet, and a filthy one can have none. Price pet hair by time (most run 30 minutes to 1.5 hours), as a separate line the client can see.

It also keeps the condition upcharge honest: heavy means dirt and stains, not hair.

How to quote it without losing the job

Ask before you quote. "When was it last detailed? Any kids, pets, smoking, spills? Can you send 3 photos: driver seat, back seat, driver floor?" Photos settle most arguments before they start.

Put the rule in writing. One line on every quote: "Price is for a standard interior. If it's heavier than the photos show, we'll show you and confirm the new price before we start." Then do exactly that.

Show, don't argue. Pull a seat back, shine a light on the carpet, and show the client what you're looking at. People pay for what they can see.

Never upcharge after the job. A surprise on the invoice loses the client, the review and the referral. Confirm the price before the first tool comes out.

Lessons that cost detailers the most

Eating the time to keep the client happy. They'll book you again at the same price, and the car will be just as dirty.

A flat "dirty fee." $50 extra on a small car and a work truck isn't the same work. A percentage scales with the job.

Calling it a penalty. It isn't. It's the hours. "This one needs about an hour and a half more, so it's $X" lands better than "there's a fee."

Taking jobs you shouldn't. Mold, needles, heavy bodily fluids: those can need protective equipment and products made for biohazards. It's fine to refer them out.
