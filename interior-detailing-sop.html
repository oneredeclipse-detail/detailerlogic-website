Process guide

Interior detailing SOP, step by step

Most interior complaints aren't about skill. A wet seat that smells like mildew two days later. A screen with a swirl in it. A slick steering wheel. A missing ring. A good process stops every one of those before it happens. Here's the order we work in and the checks that make an interior hard to argue with.

Updated November 19, 2026 · 9-minute read · Printable checklist at the bottom

Why an SOP beats talent

When you're tired on the fourth car of the day, you skip things. An SOP is the list that doesn't get tired. It also lets you hand a car to a helper and know what comes back, and it gives you something to point to when a client asks what they paid for.

Top to bottom. Dry before wet. Test before you trust. Dry before you leave.

That's the whole system in one line. Everything below is those four rules applied in order.

1. Intake: protect yourself first

The first ten minutes decide whether the job goes smoothly or turns into a dispute.

  1. Walk the car with the client.

    Ask what bothers them most: a stain, a smell, the pet hair. That's the thing they'll check first when you're done. Confirm the condition and the price before any tool comes out, including pet hair and odor, which are priced as add-ons. (How to price condition: how to charge for a dirty interior.)

  2. Take "before" photos.

    Every seat, the floors, the dash, the headliner and anything already damaged: tears, burns, cracked trim, worn leather, a stain that won't come out. Photos settle "that wasn't there before" in ten seconds.

  3. Note how they left it.

    Seat position, mirrors, steering wheel, radio station. Snap one photo of the driver's area. Putting it all back is the kind of detail clients mention in reviews.

  4. Empty it and bag the valuables.

    Check every pocket, console, door bin and under every seat. Put anything that isn't trash in a labeled bag and hand it back at the end. Never throw out paperwork, receipts or anything you're unsure about.

  5. Mats out, and cleaned first.

    Pull them and clean them outside the car right away, then set them to dry while you work. Carpet mats take the longest, so they go first. They go back in last, fully dry.

  6. Know when to stop.

    Mold, needles and heavy bodily fluids are a stop. Don't start. Photograph what you see, tell the client, then decline or reprice for the right protective gear and products, or refer it out.

2. Dry work: get the loose stuff out

Adding water to loose dirt makes mud. Get the dry stuff out first. Which path you take depends on the condition you priced at intake.

Heavy loose dirt? Pre-vacuum first. Otherwise, blow out first.

Standard interior (light, clean-ish): blow out, pet hair if any, then straight to wet work. The vacuum comes after the wet work.
Heavy, Restore or Extreme (lots of sand, crumbs, debris): vacuum the loose debris first, then blow out and pet hair, then a full vacuum before any wet work.

  1. Heavy only: vacuum the loose debris first.

    Take the bulk out of the seats and floors. Blowing out a heavy interior without this coats every plastic surface in dust you then have to clean twice.

  2. Blow out the seams, vents and carpet.

    Compressed air or a blower from the top down: vents, seat seams, between the seats and console, under the seats, and into the carpet to lift what's packed in. Work toward the doors. Wear eye and ear protection and a dust mask. Blowouts throw fine dust and the air tools are loud.

  3. Pet hair, if there is any.

    Take it out before any water goes on. Wet hair weaves into the carpet and is far harder to pull. This is the add-on you confirmed at intake. (How to price pet hair.)

  4. Heavy only: full vacuum before wet work.

    Headliner edges and visors, then seats, then carpet, under the seats and the trunk. Move the seats all the way forward, then back, so you get both halves of the floor. A stiff brush on the carpet ahead of the nozzle lifts packed-in dirt and sand.

3. Wet work: the right product, the least water

Test first, every time. Before a new cleaner goes on a surface, try it on a hidden spot (under the seat, inside the lower door) and wipe it off. Watch for color on the towel, haze or swelling. Ten seconds of testing is cheaper than a seat.

Work one quarter of the car at a time, in the same order on every car: [QUARTER ORDER]. In each quarter, put cleaner on the worst spots first (cup holders, console, a stained seat) and let it dwell while you work the rest. Then go top down, carpet last, and close out the quarter with its door and jamb.

  1. Headliner: towel only.

    Never spray the headliner. Spray the cleaner onto a microfiber and blot, don't scrub. Soaking it can let go of the glue, and a sagging headliner is a costly fix.

  2. Dash, console, doors, vents and cup holders.

    Spray the towel or brush, not the surface, so nothing drips into switches or the screen. Use a cleaner made for interiors at the dilution on the label. Many automakers warn against alcohol, ammonia, peroxide and degreasers on painted and soft-touch trim, so don't reach for them there. Wipe dry right after.

  3. Screens and clusters.

    A clean, dry or barely damp microfiber, light pressure. Never spray anything directly onto a screen, and skip glass cleaners with ammonia or alcohol unless the automaker says they're fine.

  4. Seatbelts.

    Pull the belt all the way out, clean it with mild soap and water, and let it dry before it retracts. No bleach or dye on belts.

  5. Seats: clean for the material.

    Cloth: mist the cleaner, agitate with a soft upholstery brush, extract, and don't flood the cushion. Leather: figure out the type first; most car leather is coated, which takes a gentle leather cleaner and a soft brush. Older or worn leather gets no hard scrubbing. Alcantara and suede-like fabrics: low moisture, a soft brush, no direct steam. The app's Playbook has a page for each surface.

    Watch: cleaning coated leather. Most car leather has a plastic topcoat, so leather conditioner just sits on top. Clean it with a gentle cleaner and a soft brush, then leave it. If you want protection, use a non-silicone protectant like Colourlock Leather Shield.

  6. Carpet last.

    Extracting it? Vacuum that section right before you extract, so loose dirt doesn't turn to mud. Then mist in controlled passes, agitate, and extract right away. Never pour or flood. Water that soaks into the pad under the carpet is what causes the mildew smell three days later. Not extracting? Leave it for the vacuum after the wet work.

  7. Door and jamb close out the quarter.

    The client sees the jambs and sills every time they get in. Clean them, wipe dry, then move to the next quarter.

Odors: find the source. A smell comes from something: a spill under a seat, a wet pad, a crumb in a seam. Clean the source first with an enzyme cleaner and give it the dwell time on the label. Skip the fragrance unless the client asks for one, because it covers the problem and comes back. Ozone only after the interior is clean and dry, with nobody and no pets in the car, and a full airing out afterward.
Seats with airbags, sensors and heaters. Many front seats have side airbags, occupant sensors and heating elements inside. Keep moisture to the minimum on front seats and anything marked with an airbag label, and never soak them. If you're unsure about a car, check the owner's manual.

4. Finish: where most complaints are made

  1. Dry it before you leave.

    An air mover on damp carpet and seats, windows cracked if it's safe, and a hand check. If it still feels damp to your palm, it isn't done. Tell the client how long to leave the windows cracked if anything is still drying.

  2. Vacuum again.

    The wet work loosens debris you couldn't reach before. Take it up now, before any dressing goes on.

  3. Dressing: matte and dry to the touch.

    Use a non-greasy interior dressing on hard plastics, console and cup holders. Never put it on pedals, the steering wheel, shifters or airbag covers, and keep it off soft-touch trim unless the product says it's safe. A slick pedal or steering wheel is a safety problem, not a finish.

  4. Inside glass and mirrors last.

    Everything else throws overspray and dust onto the glass, so it goes last. Two towels: one to clean, one to buff.

  5. Mats back in, fully dry.

    Hook them onto the factory clips. Never stack a mat on top of another one on the driver's side; it can slide under the pedals.

  6. Final check.

    A smell check with the doors shut, seats and mirrors back where the client had them, valuables bag on the passenger seat or in their hand. Every door shut, dome lights off, windows up (or cracked on purpose), nothing left inside: no towel, brush or bottle.

  7. After photos and the walkthrough.

    Same angles as the before photos. Then show the client the thing they told you bothered them most. That one spot is most of the review. On a full detail, do this after the exterior step below.

5. Full detail: interior first, then the exterior

On a full detail we do the interior before the exterior wash. The mats get a head start drying, and the interior isn't left with water spots and drips from jambs and doors.

  1. Interior, then exterior.

    Finish sections 1 to 4 first. The mats dry while you wash.

  2. Re-wipe after the wash.

    Door jambs, sills and the inside of the glass pick up drips and dust during the wash. A quick wipe with a clean microfiber catches it.

  3. One last vacuum pass.

    Then mats in, doors shut, and photos and the walkthrough. The client's first look should be the car at its best.

Lessons that cost detailers the most

Starting before the price is confirmed. Once the vacuum is running, the conversation about a heavier interior gets harder. Confirm first.

No before photos. The one time you skip them is the time a client says the tear in the seat is new.

Too much water. More water doesn't mean cleaner. It means a wet pad, a long dry and a smell callback.

Shine where it doesn't belong. Greasy dash dressing glares on the windshield and attracts dust; slick pedals are dangerous. Matte and dry wins.

Taking jobs you shouldn't. Mold, needles and heavy bodily fluids can need protective equipment and products made for biohazards. It's fine to refer them out, and it's the right call.
