Pricing guide

How to price a ceramic coating

Most coating prices come from what the shop across town charges or what the bottle costs. Neither one counts the hours of prep that make a coating last. Here's how we price coatings so the most skilled job you do pays like it.

Updated November 11, 2026 · 6-minute read

What a cheap coating really pays you

A detailer quotes $500 to coat a sedan. Before the coating goes on, the paint needs a wash, decon and a careful one-step polish, then the install and the wipe-downs.

Prep + one-step polish8 hrs
Coating install3 hrs
$500 − $100 coating product$400
$400 ÷ 11 hours$36 an hour

That's before towels, pads, compound and the comeback when a high spot shows up a week later. Price the same job at $100 an hour and it's $1,100 for the time plus $100 for the product: $1,200. That's $700 left on the table, on the most skilled job you do.

A coating is only as good as the prep under it. Price the prep.

Step 1: See the paint before you quote

You can't price correction from a photo. Swirls, deep scratches, thin clear coat, old repaint: each changes the hours. Look at it in good light, take a paint reading if you can, and do a test spot. Until then, quote a range: "Coatings on a sedan start at $X, final price after we see the paint."

Step 2: Count the correction hours

Typical ranges for a sedan, start to finish. Your times will vary with your tools and the paint:

Prep levelWhat it isSedan, about
EnhancementWash, decon, light polish for gloss3–4 hrs
One-stepRemoves most light swirls6–8 hrs
Two-stepCompound then polish, deeper defects12–15 hrs

For a coating you put your name on, start at a one-step. Bigger vehicles take longer. Multiply the hours the same way you would for any detail (an SUV about 1.2×, a full-size truck about 1.5×).

Step 3: Add the install and the product

Install, leveling, wipe-downs and cure checks usually add 2 to 4 hours. Then add the coating at what it costs you, plus whatever else the job eats: pads, compound, towels. Don't mark your time down to cover product. Product is its own line.

Step 4: Sell three tiers, not one price

Coating and correction are skilled work, and you stand behind the result for years. Many detailers charge a higher hourly rate for it than for interiors; our example uses $100 an hour. Most clients don't know what they want until they see options, so offer three tiers, priced by the hours each really takes. Example on a sedan, with example product costs:

TierHoursPrice
2-year: one-step + coating8 + 3 = 11$1,100 + $100 = $1,200
5-year: heavier one-step + coating9 + 4 = 13$1,300 + $150 = $1,450
7-year: two-step + coating14 + 4 = 18$1,800 + $200 = $2,000

The longer a coating is rated, the better the prep under it has to be, and the more you're standing behind it. That's why the top tier carries the two-step. Most clients pick the middle, which is exactly where you want them.

Step 5: Turn the coating into repeat work

A coated car still needs washing, and it needs the right wash. Book a maintenance wash with every coating: every month, or every few months. It protects the coating (and your name on it), and it turns one big job into a client for years.

Lessons that cost detailers the most

Pricing off the bottle. The coating is the cheapest part of the job. Your hours are the expensive part.

Quoting before seeing the paint. A "$500 coating" on paint that needs a two-step is an 18-hour job at about $22 an hour.

Coating over defects to save time. The coating locks them in for years, with your name on it.

No maintenance plan. The client washes it at the tunnel wash, it looks bad in six months, and the coating gets the blame.
