How much to charge for pet hair removal

Pet hair is the job nobody wants to quote, so it gets tossed in for free or buried in the price. Either way you lose the hour. Here's how we price it so it pays for the time it takes, and the client sees exactly why.

Updated November 16, 2026 · 4-minute read

What "just a little hair" really costs you

A client books a $225 interior. When you open the doors, the back seat and cargo area are covered in dog hair woven into the carpet. You say "no problem" and spend an extra hour and a half getting it out.

Extra time on the hair: 1.5 hrs × $75 an hour$112.50
Charged for it$0

That's $112.50 of your time given away. Do it three times a week and it adds up to $16,875 a year. Same chemicals, same truck, same you, working for free.

Pet hair is time. Price it by the hour, on its own line.

Why a flat "pet hair fee" doesn't work

A $25 fee is too much for a few strands on one seat and not nearly enough for a husky's winter coat. Either the client with a little hair feels cheated, or you lose money on the heavy ones. The only number that stays honest is how long it takes you.

Step 1: See the hair before you quote

Ask for two photos with the booking: the back seat and the cargo area. Hair hides in the carpet and the seat seams, so you want to see how it's sitting, not just how much there is. A quote made without seeing it is a guess, and you'll eat the difference.

Step 2: Sort it into four levels

Use what you can see, not what the client says. "A little hair" means something different to everyone.

Light: a few spots, loose, comes up with a vacuum (30 min)$37.50
Moderate: a full seat or cargo area, some woven in (1 hr)$75.00
Heavy: several areas, woven into carpet (1.5 hrs)$112.50
Extreme: wall-to-wall, quote after you see it (2+ hrs)by the hour

Those are examples at $75 an hour. Use your own rate and your own times. The point is that you have levels, so you aren't making up a number every time.

Step 3: Price it as its own line

Don't fold it into the interior price or into your condition level. A spotless interior can still have a coat of hair in the carpet, and a filthy one can have none. A separate line, "Pet hair removal, $75," shows the client what the work is, and they can see why the price moved. If you use condition levels, see how to charge for a dirty interior.

Step 4: Use the right tools, so the hours stay short

The faster you are, the better your hourly rate. A rubber pet hair brush or rake pulls hair out of carpet. Compressed air lifts it out of seams. A pumice-style stone works on stubborn spots, tested somewhere hidden first so it doesn't fuzz the fabric. Then vacuum last. A quote doesn't have to be padded, it just has to be for the time the job takes you.

Step 5: Say it before they book

Put it on your booking page and price list: "Pet hair removal starts at $40 and depends on how much there is. Send us a photo for an exact price." A client who knew about it before booking isn't surprised on the invoice. Never add it after the work is done.

Lessons that cost detailers the most

Tossing it in for free to be nice. It trains clients to expect it, and the hour comes out of your pay.

One flat fee for every car. Too high for light hair, too low for heavy.

Quoting without photos. You find out how bad it is with the doors open and the client standing there.

Burying it in the interior price. The client can't see what they're paying for, so your price looks high next to someone who left it out.
