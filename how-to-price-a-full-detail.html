How to price a full detail: the hourly method

Most detailers price a full detail by looking at what the shop down the street charges. That's how a 5-hour job ends up paying $36 an hour. Here's the method we've used for over 30 years: start with your hour, and let the car set the price.

Updated November 9, 2026 · 6-minute read

The problem with one flat SUV price

Say you charge $180 for a full detail on a mid-size SUV. A thorough job takes you 5 hours, start to finish.

Price$180
Hours on the job5
What that job pays you per hour$36

Before chemicals, gas, insurance and the days it rains. And a flat price treats a garage-kept SUV the same as one with a winter of salt on the carpet and dog hair in every seam, so the dirty ones pay you even less.

Now price that same job at $75 an hour: 5 hours is $375. That's $195 left on the table, every time. Do four of those a week for 50 weeks and it adds up to $39,000 a year you worked for and never got paid.

Price the hour, not the vehicle. The vehicle tells you how many hours.

Step 1: Set what your hour is worth

Your hourly rate isn't what you pay yourself. It has to cover your products, fuel, insurance, equipment wear, the hours you spend quoting and driving, and the days you don't work. Work it out once, then use it on every job.

Not sure where to start? Divide what you charge for your most common job by the hours it really takes. If that number can't cover the list above and still pay you, your rate is the problem, not your speed. Pick your number and stick to it.

Step 2: Time the job honestly

Count the whole job: setup, every step, wipe-down, final walk-around and pack-up. If you're mobile, add the drive there and back.

A real full detail on a mid-size SUV, done right with no shortcuts, takes most solid detailers 4 to 5 hours. If yours takes 7, the price isn't the problem. Getting faster is part of your raise.

Step 3: Adjust for vehicle size

Bigger vehicles take longer. Instead of a separate price list for every size, multiply:

Step 4: Price the inside and outside separately

This is where most detailers lose money, or lose the job. A trashed interior is a lot more work, but it doesn't make the paint any dirtier. If you add a "heavy soil" charge to the whole job, you either overcharge for the exterior or swallow the extra interior time.

Charge for condition on the part that needs it. Same mid-size SUV at $75 an hour: interior 2.5 hours and heavy, exterior 2 hours and normal, plus pet hair removal.

Interior: 2.5 hrs × $75 × 1.2 × 1.25 (heavy)$281.25
Exterior: 2 hrs × $75 × 1.2 (normal)$180.00
Pet hair: 1 hr × $75 × 1.2$90.00
Total$551.25

Mark the whole job heavy and it comes to $596.25. That extra $45 is the kind of number that makes a client shop around. Priced by part, the price matches the work, and you can explain every line.

Step 5: Price add-ons on their own

Pet hair, headlight restoration, odor treatment, engine bay. Keep them as separate lines, priced by time (or a set price for jobs that always take the same time, like headlights). The client sees what they're paying for, and a "yes" to one add-on is an easy upsell on the spot.

Lessons that cost detailers the most

Copying the competition's price. You don't know their hours, their costs or whether they're making money.

Discounting to win the job. The clients who need a discount to book are often the first to leave for a cheaper price.

One price per vehicle type. Condition changes the hours more than size does. Price the condition (how to charge for a dirty interior).

Forgetting the drive. Mobile time is billable time. Charge travel by distance past your free miles.
